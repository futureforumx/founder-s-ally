# Move 4 — The advice-first conversation

The first call has one purpose: the founder ends it thinking *"we probably should be doing this."* Not *"that person wants a job."*

That ordering matters more than anything else in this method. If the founder decides the conversation was a disguised pitch, they re-read every earlier signal as manipulation, and the trust the user spent weeks building is gone in a sentence.

## Structure

**1. Why this market (60 seconds).** Genuine reason for the interest, plus enough evidence of homework that they relax — they're not on a sales call. Name what the user looked at: competitors, the funnel they walked through, the numbers they estimated.

**2. Present the pattern, not the prescription.** What the user found happening in the market, with specifics. Who's doing it, how it appears to be working, why it seems to fit this kind of product.

**3. Ask why they're not doing it.** This is the pivotal question, and it has to be asked as real curiosity, because it is: the founder may know something that kills the idea.

> "Most of your closest competitors run this. You're not, and I'd assume that's deliberate — is there a reason it doesn't work for you?"

**4. Listen properly.** The answer routes everything after it:

- *"We tried it, it didn't work."* Find out exactly how — who ran it, what budget, over what period, what they measured. Failed attempts are often abandoned experiments, not verdicts. But if it genuinely failed for a structural reason, believe them and update; arguing with data they have and the user doesn't ends credibility.
- *"Our market is different because…"* Possibly true, and worth learning. Ask what would have to be true for it to work.
- *"We've thought about it, never had the time."* The best answer available. Keep going.
- *"We have someone on it."* Ask how it's going. Sometimes "someone" is a part-time contractor with no ownership, which is still an opening. Sometimes it's closed — accept it and ask what they're actually short of.

**5. Walk through the money.** Their product, their numbers, the mechanism, the rough upside. Show the competitor cases. Stay in *if this works for you* framing, not *here's what I'd do for you*.

**6. Stop there.** Thank them for the read, offer to send the research, ask the one thing that keeps the door open: *"who else in this market should I be talking to?"* Then end the call.

## What not to say on call one

- "I could do this for you." The single most common way this method fails.
- "Are you hiring?"
- Anything about their team being too small, too slow, or missing something. The gap is an opportunity, not a criticism.
- Any claim the user can't back. Founders test claims later, often with people the user knows.
- Overconfident certainty about a market the founder has lived in for years. Confidence in the method, humility about their specifics.

## Why stopping works

After the call, the founder is thinking about the opportunity — and in most cases discovers they either have nobody to run it or no time to run it themselves. At that moment, they already know exactly one person who's researched this problem, understands their market, and has done it before.

That's a much stronger position than being the person who asked for work in minute nine. Frequently the founder raises it first, which changes the entire negotiation.

## After the call

Send a short note within a day: two or three lines of thanks, the competitor examples and any numbers, nothing asked for. Concrete and useful, so it reinforces the peer frame rather than reopening a pitch.

Then leave it alone for a few days to a week. If they don't come back, move 5 has a low-pressure way to reopen it — but the gap between call and offer is what does the work.

## Handling the founder who asks directly

If they ask "would you want to work on this?" — that's the outcome, don't flinch and don't oversell. Straight answer, then hand them move 5:

> "Yeah, I'd be interested in testing it. I'd rather prove it than get hired on a promise — let me put together what a small test would look like and you can see if it's worth it."

## Output

A one-page call plan: the opening, the pattern with its specific evidence, the why-not question in their exact words, the branch responses to the four likely answers, the two cases to show, the one line not to say, and the closing question. Written to be glanced at mid-call — bullets, not paragraphs.
