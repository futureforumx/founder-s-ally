# Move 1 — Finding the edge

The goal of this move is a sentence the user can say out loud that makes a founder think *"oh, that's rare."* Not a job title. Not a stack. A thing they do better than most people, with receipts.

## Why self-assessment fails here

People are bad at naming their own edge for two reasons. They over-weight what they enjoy, and they under-weight anything that felt easy to them — which is exactly where edges live. Something that took them a weekend and took everyone else two months reads to them as "not a real skill."

So work from evidence and from other people's observations, not from introspection.

## The extraction interview

Ask for episodes, not adjectives. Adjectives ("I'm strategic," "I'm a fast learner") carry no information. Episodes do.

Ask these in the round format from SKILL.md — numbered, all at once, each with your best guess attached where the earlier conversation gives you one to make.

Useful prompts:

- What's the last thing you did where someone else's number went up? Which number, how much?
- What have people asked you to help with more than once — including things you weren't paid for?
- What do you end up fixing on every team you join, regardless of your actual role?
- What's something you finished in a weekend that others estimated in weeks?
- Where have you been the person who unblocked something nobody else could?
- What's the most money you've directly caused to exist, and what did you do?
- What do you know about a market, tool, or audience that most people in it get wrong?

Push on vague answers exactly once, with specificity: "which product, what changed, over what period?" If the numbers don't exist, don't invent proxies — note it as an evidence gap and handle it in `evidence-building.md`.

## Turning episodes into an edge

Look across the episodes for the repeated mechanism, not the repeated job title. Someone whose episodes are "grew a meme page to 400k," "wrote the launch thread that got a product 9k signups," and "fixed a friend's onboarding flow and doubled activation" doesn't have an edge in "marketing." They have an edge in *making cold consumer audiences take a first action*. That's specific enough to be valuable and to be targeted.

Test each candidate edge against three questions:

1. **Is it narrow enough to be believable?** "Growth" isn't. "Getting consumer apps from zero to their first 10k users through organic short-form video" is.
2. **Does someone make money when it works?** If you can't trace a line from the skill to revenue, it's a hobby in this context, however impressive.
3. **Is there proof?** A number, a shipped thing, a named person who watched it happen.

End with 1–3 candidate edges. More than three means the extraction wasn't sharp enough.

## Validating with other humans

Self-validated edges are worth little. Have the user ask 5–8 people who actually worked with them — and make the questions open, because closed questions just harvest agreement.

Don't ask: *"Am I good at marketing?"* That's a request to confirm a belief, and people are polite.

Do ask:

- What stood out about working with me?
- When did you think I handled something unusually well?
- What would you specifically come to me for, over other people you know?
- What did I make easier for you?
- If you were describing me to someone hiring, what would you say?

Draft these as a message the user can paste, in their voice, to specific named people — ex-managers, cofounders, clients, teammates. Then read the answers with them and look for the words that repeat across people. Those words are the edge, and they're often not the words the user would have chosen.

## Testing competing hypotheses

If two or three candidate edges survive, don't debate them — test them. Cheap tests, roughly in order of speed:

- Write the same offer three ways (one per edge) and see which gets replies from the same kind of company
- Post a short breakdown of a problem in each domain and watch which one gets DMs and "how did you do that" questions
- Mention each in the first three conversations and notice which one makes founders lean in and ask follow-ups

Interest from the right kind of person is the signal. Applause from the wrong kind isn't.

## "I can do everything"

Treat this as an honest statement of a different asset: time and willingness. That's genuinely worth something — but only when pointed at a specific need, and it prices the user far lower than an edge does.

Two moves out of it:

1. **Borrow a constraint.** Pick the one domain where they have the most raw material (any past episode, any audience, any unusual access) and commit to it for a fixed window — six weeks, say. Breadth becomes an edge only after depth gives it somewhere to attach.
2. **Convert generality into a concrete, scoped promise.** Not "I can help with anything," but "I'll run your outbound experiments for six weeks and hand you a working channel or a clear no." Generalists win on *ownership of an unowned problem*; name the problem.

## Output

Fill the positioning brief in SKILL.md. Two things people usually skip and shouldn't:

- **Evidence behind it** — with who can vouch, by name. This is what makes the later outreach work.
- **What to stop claiming** — every unprovable line the user drops makes the provable ones louder. Be direct about which claims are hurting them.
